The problem

A complex quote isn't a rate. It's a puzzle assembled over email.

For a container, a forwarder looks up a rate. For a 186-tonne transformer there is no rate to look up. The price is built leg by leg, each from a different supplier, each with its own conditions, exclusions and validity.

A simple test: if your team asks three or more suppliers to answer one request, Argo is built for you.

Gaps surface late

A supplier quietly excludes the route survey. Two carriers quote on different terms. It shows up while writing the offer, or after.

How the product works

Three parts, all working on the email the team already sends.

Customers and suppliers don't change anything. Argo connects to the forwarder's mailbox and works next to the operator.

1 · Memory

The whole mailbox as context

Argo reads every email and attachment, past and present. Years of quotes become something it can use.

  • Similar past RFQs and what they cost
  • How each supplier behaves: what they exclude, how fast they answer
  • Which tenders were lost, and why
2 · Chat

One conversation per RFQ

The operator works each request with Argo, in plain language.

  • Flags what doesn't match between email and attachments
  • Drafts questions, supplier requests and the offer
  • Lines up offers that aren't like-for-like
  • Prepares a handover when someone is away
3 · Quote

A quote that fills in as decisions are made

Each cost line shows where it stands and which email it came from.

The operator decides. Argo prepares.

Argo never sends on its own and never puts a price or a scope change into the quote without the operator's confirmation. What it tracks by itself are facts it sees in the mail: who was asked, who replied, what was sent.

A case, start to finish

Two power transformers, Italy to a substation in Chile.

A fictional request with the complexity of real ones: four transport modes, five suppliers, a customer who changes the scope, and a problem that appears after the offer is sent.

2 Mar

The RFQ arrives from an EPC contractor

An email and three attachments: datasheet, drawing, tender terms. Argo reads them together and finds three mismatches: operating weight against transport weight, height, and the Incoterm. The questions deadline is two weeks before the offer.

Argo3 mismatches · 2 similar RFQs found in the mailbox
4 Mar

The customer adds a spare unit

Most answers clarify. One changes the scope: an optional third transformer, shipping a year later. Argo logs it as a change, with the email as proof, and adds an optional cost line.

ArgoChange logged · optional line added
4–5 Mar

Five requests go out in parallel

Heavy haulier and barge operator in Italy, two heavy-lift carriers, a destination agent in Chile. The route survey is asked for by name, because this agent left it out last time.

Argo5 requests drafted · 8 cost lines tracked
12 Mar

Two carrier offers that don't compare

One carrier quotes liner terms with loading included, the other FIOS lashed and secured, with a different laycan and the crates left out. Argo lays out the differences and drafts the questions to close them.

Argo4 differences found · like-for-like questions drafted
19 Mar

Offer sent, exclusions in writing

Every service the customer asked for is priced or excluded by name. The route survey isn't back yet, so road costs go out as indicative, subject to survey. Validity follows the shortest supplier validity.

ArgoOffer drafted from confirmed lines only
26–27 Mar

The survey finds a bridge, then a handover

A bridge at km 92 can't take the load: a 38 km detour with extra escorts. The cost goes to the customer as a dated revision. The operator then goes on leave, and one message hands the case to a colleague with what's decided, open and pending.

ArgoRevision logged · handover ready
May

Awarded

The EPC awards the move. Argo prepares the job data for the forwarder's management system and keeps the case in memory: the next transformer RFQ starts from here.
